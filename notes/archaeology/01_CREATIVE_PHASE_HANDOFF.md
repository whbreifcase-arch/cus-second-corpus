# CREATIVE PHASE HANDOFF

**You have not seen the source material, and you will not.** This document is
everything you get. It is deliberately short.

You are about to design a game. What follows was reconstructed from several years of
one designer's work — rulebooks, revisions, simulations, abandoned systems, arguments
with himself. Your job is not to reproduce that work. Your job is to serve the intent
underneath it.

Nothing here describes a mechanic. That is on purpose.

---

## 1 · DESIGN CONSTITUTION

### Mission
A miniatures game that a family can play tonight on a coffee table, in about an hour,
with figures they already own and no referee — and that remembers what happened.

### Player Promise
You take people out, you bring some of them back changed, and what happened to them
was partly your fault. Nothing here scores a kill. The animals always come home.

### Core Fantasy
Stewardship on a green road, and the cost of it.

### Core Gameplay Loop
**Read the board → commit → live with where you are standing → write one line about it
→ carry it to the next fight.**

### Design Values
1. The table is the memory.
2. Committing forecloses something.
3. The battlefield decides, not the character sheet.
4. Judged by eye, settled in seconds.
5. Say what a thing *is*, never what it is *for*.
6. Depth comes from the board, never from a table of combinations.

### Acceptable Tradeoffs
- **Precision, wherever it costs time.** Honest judgment and a coin beat measurement.
- **Simulation fidelity, wherever it costs a decision.**
- **Universality.** This is a skirmish game that remembers. It is not a grammar for all
  organised action.
- **Internal perfection.** A subsystem may ship switched off with its hooks printed.

### Explicit Non-Goals
- Cross-faction points balance. Asymmetry is content; the scenario balances the match.
- A referee, an AI director, or any second scripted mind.
- Bespoke components as a requirement to play.
- A rules corpus that is correct before it is played.
- Cross-setting content before one setting is finished.
- Any procedure for redemption, or for what a person is worth.

## 2 · DESIGN DNA

Ten immutable principles. Each was independently re-derived across designs that shared
no vocabulary and no resolution engine. Treat them as findings, not preferences.

1. **The table is the memory.** Nothing persistent lives in a head. A player holds
   only what is true of the figure their hand is touching, right now.
2. **Committing forecloses something.** Every good mechanic in this design is one idea
   in a different costume: you get one commitment, and taking it closes a door. Use it
   as a test — *does this proposal create a commitment with a real foreclosure, or is
   it just a modifier?*
3. **The battlefield decides, not the character sheet.** Where a rule wants to limit a
   figure, deny it the geometry, not a budget. If the advantage of position is not
   worth more than the entire spread of the character sheet, position is not the
   primary language and the rules are lying.
4. **Nobody at the table is the referee.** The enemy runs itself off something
   printed. Every branch ends in a line that always works, and there is a coin at the
   bottom of everything.
5. **Judged by eye, settled in seconds.** Looks reachable, it's reachable. Five
   minutes on one activation is a fault, not diligence.
6. **A rule is wrong if it fails the youngest player or the clock.** Those are the two
   judges. The second one does not exist on paper.
7. **Loss must be bearable and it must be real.** Falling is not dying, and who lives
   is the story's business. Death is permanent, and that is the only reason surviving
   means anything.
8. **The enemy is a person, and killing is a choice the rules refuse to score.**
9. **Being alone is the worst thing that can happen to someone.** Bodies heal with
   care and time. Minds heal with other people.
10. **Depth comes from the board.** Say what a thing is; never say what it is for.

## 3 · PLAYER PROMISE (expanded)

**Who is at the table.** Two to four people on a coffee table or a two-foot square,
for about an hour, with miniatures already in the house and coins for wound tokens. At
least one is a child — young enough that the darkest options are held back for older
players, and old enough to hold veto power over any rule too slow or too confusing.
A second adult plays half the figures and reads the other side's cards, because there
is no referee and nobody should have to sit out.

**What they are promised.** Not conquest. Not tactical mastery. A road, some people
you are responsible for, and consequences you can point at afterwards.

**What they should remember years later.** The first time someone did not come home,
and the animals did. The evening there was one bed and two broken bodies. The argument
about whether to kill the enemy's gravedigger. Sending a helper in first, knowing he
eats the answer, so the blade behind him lands unanswered. Writing one sentence about
a scar and finding that the sentence *is* the mechanic.

---

## 4 · EMOTIONAL GOALS

1. **Loss reversible enough to bear, permanent enough to matter.**
2. **A death should be a decision someone made, not a number that came up.**
3. **The enemy should be someone.**
4. **Being alone should be the worst outcome.**
5. **Play should generate a written record, and the record should be the mechanic.**
6. **A veteran should be visibly changed, not merely worse.** Every scar takes
   something and gives something, because that is what surviving does.
7. **The room should have to argue, out loud, about something the rules refuse to
   settle.** When a group works something out at their own table, what they worked out
   is theirs.
8. **A young player should be safe here, and should be able to grow out of it without
   leaving.**

---

## 5 · NON-NEGOTIABLE PRINCIPLES

- **Two judges rule every fight: the youngest player at the table, and the clock.** Any
  rule too slow or too confusing for either is wrong, and is simplified on the spot.
- **Nobody at the table is the referee.**
- **No number is ever added to a die at the table.**
- **Falling is not dying. Death is permanent.**
- **A rule you cannot see on the table is not a rule.**
- **Nothing scores a kill.**
- **The system never claims to have modelled the whole of a person.** Some things are
  left blank on purpose, and a helpful definition of them is a violation, not an
  improvement.

## 6 · FALSE CONSTRAINTS

Assumptions inherited from the previous work that look like laws and are not. You are
released from all of them. Several are actively harmful.

1. **"Nothing may be carried forward merely because it exists."** The most expensive
   assumption in the history of this project. It guarantees that every rebuild costs
   more and yields less. Nothing migrates merely because it existed — **but nothing is
   rebuilt merely because it is old.** Carrying forward is the default for anything
   that has been played.
2. **"The vocabulary must be correct before content exists."** Content is what
   generates the pressure that makes a vocabulary correct. Build content first, once,
   and see what the words have to become.
3. **"The campaign layer requires software."** The only persistence mechanic that has
   ever survived is one die roll and one written sentence.
4. **"The engine must carry politics, trade, logistics and exploration as peers."** It
   does not. It has never had to. Combat is the only thing that was ever finished.
5. **"Multiple settings from the start."** Setting-neutral content is why there is no
   content. Finish one.
6. **"A reserved blank must be a formal primitive."** The principle belongs in the
   constitution. It does not need a node in a graph, a tripwire, and a custodian.
7. **"A refereeless enemy needs its own symbol grammar."** The commitment to a
   refereeless enemy is real and permanent. The symbol grammar is not — plain English
   did the same job, in fewer words, without collisions.
8. **"An exception must be a named field, never prose."** Good, and incomplete. A
   field that nothing reads is not an exception; it is a note.
9. **"Integrity is validation."** Checking that files exist is not checking that
   content is legal. Lint the numbers, not just the words.
10. **"Each artifact must be internally perfect before the next begins."** A complete,
    playable game once shipped here with an entire subsystem switched off and its
    sockets left visible. That is allowed.

## 7 · KNOWN RISKS

1. **The rebuild reflex.** This design has been rebuilt from scratch five or six times
   in five weeks. Every rebuild was justified by a real defect and every rebuild
   produced less playable material than the one before it. **If you find yourself
   fixing the vocabulary, stop and go to the table instead.**
2. **Nothing here has ever been played.** Every number you inherit is analysis, not
   observation. Treat all of it as a hypothesis.
3. **Architecture crowds out content.** In the most recent attempt, governance
   outweighed the material it governed by more than two to one, and the content folders
   were empty.
4. **Position saturates.** When damage is a small integer, health pools are small, and
   the result ladder is short, base lethality can exceed the health pool — at which
   point extra dice for a better position are worth almost nothing, no matter what the
   constitution says. **Compute the payoff of your best position against your standard
   figure before you write the law.**
5. **Floors quietly delete penalties.** A "never below one die" rule makes every
   negative modifier a no-op for anything that rolls one die. Check what fraction of
   your actual roster that is.
6. **Discrete result bands can be non-monotonic.** If a higher band does not inherit a
   lower one, you can write a better result that is worse. Lint for it; it has already
   happened three times.
7. **A dominated option is an invisible deletion.** If one weapon is strictly worse
   than another, every mechanic that hangs off it — including counterplay — silently
   never fires.
8. **The emotional layer is unbuilt and keeps being scheduled.** Bonds, recruitment,
   mercy accounting, the age gate. Each has been promised in many documents and begun
   in none. It is the most valuable unbuilt thing here, and the easiest to postpone
   forever.
9. **Tone dies in schema.** The prose voice is a real asset and it is the first thing
   lost when the work turns architectural. The most recent artifact contains no fiction
   at all.
10. **A guard that lives inside the thing it guards protects nothing.** Every
    continuity mechanism built here was abandoned by the next rebuild, which is the only
    transition this project actually performs.

## 8 · OPEN QUESTIONS

Do not invent answers to these. They mark missing knowledge, and several can only be
answered by a person or a table, not by reasoning.

1. Has any version of this game ever actually been played? No record exists.
2. Which of the two prior design lines is the product?
3. What happened in the nine-day gap where the project changed direction?
4. Is the campaign layer three pages or a software product? Both have been asserted.
5. What, concretely, is the age gate — a content rating, an in-fiction protection rule,
   or a deck filter?
6. Is a tempo penalty for melee (attack, and your next turn is shorter) survivable at
   the table? A hero ability was parked pending exactly this test.
7. Does a mutual charge break the finding that denying the run-up is the strongest
   defensive act?
8. What replaces the job that cheap bodies used to do — spending the enemy's one
   answer — now that the answer is unlimited?
9. Is there a losing condition? There has never been a way to lose the whole thing.
10. Who else is at this table, and do they want to play?
